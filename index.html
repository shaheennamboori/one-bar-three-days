“Only a pull-up bar” means it is your only piece of equipment; you will also exercise on the floor. Pulling alone cannot cover your chest and legs. This plan adds bodyweight pushes, knee-bending leg work, hip extension, and calf work.

Your starting point: two strict pull-ups. Start with one per set, not repeated maximum-effort doubles. Your push-up and leg strength are unknown: choose the easier versions when needed.

Effort rule: finish each set feeling you could do another 1–2 good reps. No grinding, swinging, or forced reps. On pull-ups, keep at least one clean rep in reserve.

Designed as a general strength starting point for an otherwise healthy adult. An injury history was not provided. Use a securely installed, load-rated bar as directed by its manufacturer; do not jump or kip on a doorway bar. Stop a movement if it causes sharp or joint pain.

02 / Make it fit

The 30-minute clock

Finish all three sets of one exercise before moving on. This is a strength session, not a nonstop circuit. Use the same schedule on all three days.

00:00–04:00Prepare
04:00–10:00Pull-ups
10:00–16:00Push-ups / pike
16:00–22:00Lunges / squats
22:00–28:00Bridges / calves
28:00–30:00Recover & log

Within every six-minute block

Start your sets at 0:00, 2:00, and 4:00 of that block. For pull-ups, that means session minutes 4, 6, and 8. Rest for the remainder of each two-minute window; the end of the block includes the transition to the next exercise.

Most floor sets take about 30–50 seconds, leaving roughly 70–90 seconds to rest. A single pull-up leaves almost two minutes. Take no more than 60 seconds per set, leaving at least 60 seconds of rest.

Preparation & recovery

First four minutes: check the bar and clear the floor; spend about a minute walking around to raise your temperature. Rehearse a few easy, shallow reps of today's floor exercises. Gently rehearse your bar grip with feet grounded if possible. Avoid a full pull-up in the warm-up: your current two-rep maximum is low.

Last two minutes: walk slowly, let breathing settle, and note your reps and exercise versions. If you still feel cold after four minutes, extend preparation and omit a work set rather than rushing.

The clock is a ceiling, not a rep target. Never keep exercising to fill a window. Use the lower end of a range if a set would take longer than a minute. If you need more recovery, skip the next set or finish with two sets; do not squeeze rest or chase missed reps. In week one, two sets per exercise at block minutes 0 and 3 are a sensible introduction if three sets leave you overly sore.

03 / Movement instructions

Know what a good rep feels like.

Use a comfortable, controlled range. Breathe out as you pull, push, or stand; breathe in as you return. Floor reps should generally take about three seconds. Each exercise below includes an easier version and a common mistake to avoid.

01 / Strict pull-up

Back · biceps · grip · core stability
  1. Take an overhand grip around shoulder width or slightly wider, thumbs wrapped around the bar. Use a comfortable chin-up grip instead if your bar permits it and it suits your joints better.
  2. Start with long arms in a comfortable hang. Brace your abdomen, keep your legs quiet, and gently engage your shoulders.
  3. Drive your elbows down and pull until your chin clears the bar without craning your neck.
  4. Lower smoothly to long arms. No leg kick, bouncing, or sudden drop. One up-and-down movement is one rep.

Your dose: one rep, then rest until the next two-minute window. Keep at least one rep in reserve.

If a single is not clean: end the bar work for today. If your bar is low enough for your feet to stay on the floor, use light leg assistance for 3–5 controlled reps instead. Do not improvise with unstable furniture.

Avoid: turning a missed rep into a jump or forced negative. A pain-free chin-up is an alternative, not an extra exercise.

Technique: NASM pull-up guide · ACE chin-up guidance. The low starting dose is tailored to your reported maximum.

02 / Push-up

Chest · triceps · front shoulders · core stability
  1. Place hands a little wider than shoulders. Extend your legs and keep a straight line from head to heels.
  2. Brace your abdomen and glutes. Bend your elbows diagonally back, about 30–45 degrees from your torso.
  3. Lower your chest toward the floor as far as you can control without shoulder pain or your hips sagging.
  4. Press the floor away and return as one unit. Use about two seconds down and one second up.

Easier: knees on the floor; keep a straight line from head through hips to knees. Choose this if six full reps would be close to failure.

Harder later: progress from knees to toes. Once full reps are comfortable, pause briefly near the bottom and reduce reps to stay within the time window.

Avoid: elbows flared sideways, hips dropping, or your chin reaching down before your chest.

Technique and modifications: NASM push-up guide.

03 / Reverse lunge

Quads · glutes · hip stability
  1. Stand with feet hip-width apart. Step one foot backward on its own track, rather than crossing behind the other leg.
  2. Bend both knees and lower your back knee toward the floor without striking it. Keep the front heel grounded and torso steady.
  3. Push through your front foot to return to standing. Keep the front knee tracking in the same direction as your toes.
  4. Alternate sides. Six on each leg means twelve total reps, not six total. Move steadily, without rushing the return.

Easier: shorten the depth and use fingertips on a wall for balance. If stepping is unstable, use a stationary split squat: keep the feet planted and bend and straighten the legs.

Harder later: increase controlled depth, then pause briefly at the bottom. Stay at or below eight per side so the whole set fits.

Avoid: balancing on a tightrope, collapsing the front knee inward, or dropping onto the back knee.

Technique: Catalyst Athletics reverse-lunge guide. This plan uses the unloaded version.

04 / Glute bridge

Glutes · hamstrings assist · trunk stability
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent and feet flat, hip-width apart. Keep arms by your sides.
  2. Gently brace your abdomen. Keep ribs down and feet close enough that your shins are roughly vertical at the top.
  3. Press through your feet and squeeze your glutes to lift the hips until shoulders, hips, and knees form a line.
  4. Hold briefly, then lower with control. Stop lifting when your hips are extended; do not seek extra height by arching your lower back.

Easier: use a smaller pain-free lift and fewer reps.

Harder later: after 3 × 15 feels comfortable, try single-leg bridges for 3 × 6–8 each side. Keep hips level; this replaces the two-leg version.

Avoid: pushing through the lower back. If hamstrings cramp, lower, reset your feet a little nearer, and reduce the range.

Technique: NASM floor-bridge guide. Single-leg progression: UCLA Health movement series.

05 / Bodyweight squat

Quads · glutes · trunk stability
  1. Stand around shoulder width, toes turned out slightly if comfortable. Hold arms in front for balance.
  2. Bend hips and knees together, lowering between your feet. Keep your whole foot grounded and knees following your toes.
  3. Go as low as you can control without pain or losing your balance. Thighs near parallel is a useful goal, not a requirement.
  4. Push through the floor and stand tall. Take roughly two seconds down and one second up.

Easier: use a shallower squat or a light fingertip touch on a wall.

Harder later: pause briefly at the bottom and return to ten reps per set. No jumping required.

Avoid: heels lifting and knees caving in. Your knees may move past your toes; control and a comfortable range matter more than forcing a vertical shin.

Squat mechanics adapted from NASM's prisoner-squat guide; arms are held forward here for balance.

06 / Standing calf raise

Calves · ankle control
  1. Stand tall on a flat floor with feet hip-width apart. Use a wall lightly for balance if needed.
  2. Keep your knees comfortably straight and rise onto the balls of both feet.
  3. Pause for about one second at the top, then lower both heels under control. Keep pressure spread across the forefoot.
  4. Repeat without bouncing or rolling onto the outside edges of your feet. One rise and return is one rep.

Easier: use a smaller heel lift with both feet.

Harder later: progress to one leg for 3 × 6–8 each side, with wall support. Complete both sides inside the one-minute work limit.

Avoid: bouncing through the Achilles tendon or treating this as a balance test. Stay on the floor; no step is needed.

Movement technique: AAOS calf-raise instructions. This plan uses its own training dose, not the rehabilitation schedule on that page.

07 / Pike push-up

Front shoulders · triceps · trunk stability
  1. Place hands just wider than shoulder width and lift your hips into an inverted V. Bend your knees a little if your hamstrings feel tight.
  2. Brace your abdomen, keep hips high, and bend your elbows diagonally back.
  3. Lower your head toward the floor in front of your hands, only as far as you can control. Keep your neck neutral; do not place weight on your head.
  4. Press through the hands and return to the V position. Take about two seconds down and one second up.

Readiness: this is more demanding than a normal push-up. Use it only if you can manage ordinary push-ups comfortably and keep shoulder control.

Easier: replace it with the knee or full push-up for 3 × 6–10. Choose this from the outset if four pike reps would be near your maximum.

Avoid: elbows flaring sideways, hips dropping into a plank, or landing on your head. No handstands or elevated feet needed.

Technique and prerequisites: NASM pike push-up guide.

04 / Getting stronger

Earn the next rep.

Run the plan for four weeks, then review. That is a review point, not a promise of a particular result. Progress only when technique, recovery, and the rep reserve stay intact.

1 / Find your version

In week one, start at the lowest reps: 1 pull-up, 6 pushes, 6 lunges per side, 10 squats, bridges, or calf raises; 4 pike reps only if ready. Choose an easier variation whenever that feels too hard.

2 / Add floor reps

When all sets are controlled with 1–2 reps left, add one rep per set next time, within the listed range. At the top of the range, use the harder version in the guide and return to lower reps. Change one thing at a time.

3 / Build the pull-up

Begin at 1 / 1 / 1. After three comfortable sessions, try 2 / 1 / 1 only when two would still leave at least one good rep in reserve. Later use 2 / 2 / 1, then 2 / 2 / 2, under the same rule. Stay at singles as long as needed.

If performance falls: keep the same dose or use two sets for the next session. Do not add negative pull-ups or extra maximum-effort tests to make up volume. If soreness changes your movement or has not settled by the next workout, take another rest day. Persistent pain needs assessment, not more reps.

Simple log: date · exercise version · set 1 / set 2 / set 3 · reps left · any discomfort. Example: “Mon — pull-up 1/1/1, about one rep left; knee push-up 6/6/6, about two left.” A notebook is enough.

05 / Why these movements

Whole-body coverage, honestly.

The bar handles pulling. Floor exercises fill the pushing and lower-body gaps. Your abdomen and lower back work mainly to stabilize the body rather than through a separate ab circuit.

The trade-off: four exercises per session give broad coverage, not equal direct training for every muscle. Calves receive less dedicated volume; hamstring knee-flexion, side deltoid, and horizontal-row work are limited. A high pull-up bar alone does not provide a safe horizontal-row setup. This is a compact general-strength foundation, not a complete bodybuilding split or a substitute for aerobic activity.

06 / Research notes

Built from guidance, adapted to you.

ACSM's 2026 guidance supports consistent resistance training, working major muscle groups at least twice weekly, using bodyweight, and not needing to train to failure. The exercise guides below inform technique. The particular exercise mix, rep ranges, pull-up progression, and 30-minute timing are a practical synthesis for your constraints—not a published protocol or a clinical prescription.
